Staying Connected in China 2026: Internet Access for Travelers

CT
City Tech Team
Updated July 24, 2026 · 10 min read
ℹ️ For travelers only
This guide is written for international travelers visiting China. The information describes publicly available commercial services and is not legal advice. Travelers are responsible for understanding and complying with local regulations. See our full disclaimer.

You land in Shanghai. You connect to airport WiFi. You open Google Maps to find your hotel. Nothing loads. You try Gmail — nothing. You try Instagram to tell your friends you arrived — nothing. You try to Google "why isn't the internet working" — and realize you can't even do that.

This is the moment every first-time visitor hits the same wall. China's internet operates differently from what you're used to, and your phone needs one small adjustment before it works the way you expect. This guide explains what's happening, what your options are, and what to do before you fly so you don't lose a day of your trip to connectivity problems.

Why your phone works differently in China

Every country operates its internet through domestic network infrastructure, and China's network routes traffic through local gateways. Some international services that you use every day — email, maps, messaging, social media — don't load when your data goes through a Chinese network connection. This isn't a problem with your phone, your SIM, or your settings. It's simply that these services aren't reachable through domestic routing.

The solution is straightforward: many travelers use a tool that routes their data through an international connection, so everything works exactly as it does at home. This tool is commonly called a connectivity service or commercial network tool, and you install it before you leave home.

Option 1: A commercial connectivity app (most reliable)

The most consistent way to keep all your apps working is a commercial connectivity app that you download, install, and test before you leave home. These are subscription services — think of them like paying for airport lounge access, but for internet. You pay a few dollars a month and your phone works normally in China.

We've tested several of the most widely used services that travelers rely on for China trips. All three below work reliably based on our tests across multiple cities and networks.

🛡️

NordVPN

Largest global network, consistently fast in our speed tests (55-60 Mbps in Shanghai), works on up to 10 devices. Their obfuscated server option is useful. Plans start ~$3.39/month on 2-year subscriptions. 30-day money-back guarantee.
⚡

ExpressVPN

Lightway protocol is notably fast (50-65 Mbps in our tests). Simple interface — good if you don't want to think about settings. Most expensive at ~$6.67/month, but reliably works on first connect. Also 30-day refund.
🔑

Surfshark

Best budget option at ~$2.19/month. Unlimited devices — install on your whole travel group. NoBorders mode covers China well. Slightly slower than Nord/Express (45-55 Mbps) but totally usable for normal email, maps, and browsing.
💡 How to choose
Speed + reliability: NordVPN or ExpressVPN (pricey but set-and-forget).
Budget: Surfshark (works fine, saves money).
Group travel: Surfshark (unlimited devices — install on everyone's phone and laptop).

Setup checklist (do this before you fly)

1
Buy a subscription

Pick one from the three above. Use the longest plan for the best price — you can refund within 30 days. Pay by credit card or PayPal.

2
Download and install the app

On your phone and laptop. Do this on your home WiFi — don't wait until you land.

3
Log in and test it

Connect to a server (Singapore, Tokyo, or Hong Kong are closest to China — fastest speeds). Confirm everything opens normally.

4
Enable auto-connect

Turn on "auto-connect on untrusted networks" in the app settings. When you join airport or hotel WiFi, it kicks in automatically.

5
Test immediately on landing

Connect to airport WiFi (free at Pudong/Capital/Daxing), confirm the app connects, and open Gmail or Google Maps to verify everything works. If it doesn't connect, switch to a different server location — that fixes 90% of launch-day issues.

⚠️ Don't use free connectivity apps
Free services are unreliable in China. They're slow, often don't connect at all, and some have been caught selling user data. For a trip you've spent hundreds or thousands on, paying $3-7 for a month of reliable connectivity is a tiny insurance premium. More importantly for travelers: free services often can't handle the connection patterns common in China, leaving you stuck without internet at exactly the wrong moment.

Option 2: eSIM with offshore routing (no separate app needed)

Some travel eSIMs route your data through Hong Kong or Singapore by default — which means apps load the same way they do at home without any additional setup. This is the simplest option if you're already buying an eSIM for data.

We detail this fully in our eSIM comparison guide, but the short version:

  • Saily — built-in security tools, one-tap setup, ~$15.99/5GB/30d
  • Nomad — cheapest per GB, best on trains, ~$10/5GB/30d
  • Holafly — unlimited data, no cap, ~$27.50/7d
ℹ️ Can I use both?
Yes. Use an eSIM for mobile data when you're out, and a connectivity app when you're on hotel/hostel WiFi. They complement each other — the eSIM handles outdoors, the app handles indoors. Also: eSIM data is metered (you have a GB limit); WiFi through the connectivity app is unlimited.

Option 3: Hotel and international-chain WiFi

International hotel chains (Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, InterContinental, Shangri-La) typically operate their own internet gateways with international routing. When you connect to their WiFi, most of your usual apps work without any additional tool. It's inconsistent — some properties have full access, others only partially — but it's worth trying before you assume you need the connectivity app.

Domestic/budget hotels are much less reliable for international internet access. If you're staying at a local chain or Airbnb, plan to use your connectivity app or eSIM.

Option 4: International roaming from your home carrier

Your home mobile carrier's international roaming plan routes data through your home country. If you have T-Mobile (US), Vodafone (UK/EU), or a similar carrier with a China roaming add-on, all your apps will work just as at home — no connectivity app needed. Check your carrier's international data rates before relying on this; roaming can be $5-15/day, which adds up fast on a 2-week trip.

What about the mobile data from a Chinese SIM card?

A physical Chinese SIM (from China Mobile/Unicom/Telecom counters at the airport) gives you fast local data at reasonable rates, but it's a domestic connection. Your usual apps, email, and mapping services won't load through it without a connectivity app. This is why we generally recommend an eSIM with offshore routing for mobile data — it solves both problems (data + app access) in one product.

What happens if you don't prepare?

If you arrive without setting anything up, you have limited options:

  • Airport WiFi works for basic browsing but likely won't load your usual apps.
  • You can buy a Chinese SIM at the airport for data, but your usual apps still won't load without a connectivity app. And you can't download a connectivity app because the app store pages for those services are not reachable on a Chinese network — which is exactly why you need to install it at home.
  • International hotel WiFi may partially work.

The takeaway is simple: set up both a connectivity app and an eSIM before you fly. Combined they cost about the same as two lunches and cover every connectivity scenario on your trip.

⚠️ Important reminder
Install everything before departure. The websites and app-store pages for connectivity services are not accessible from inside China. You cannot set this up after you land. Take 15 minutes at home the night before you fly — it's the single most valuable prep you can do.

Pre-departure checklist

  1. Buy and install a connectivity app (NordVPN, ExpressVPN, or Surfshark — whichever fits your budget)
  2. Test it: connect to a Singapore/Hong Kong/Tokyo server, open Gmail, confirm it loads
  3. Enable auto-connect on untrusted networks
  4. Buy and install an eSIM with offshore routing for mobile data outdoors
  5. Save hotel WiFi password and Chinese address offline
  6. Land, connect to WiFi, confirm app auto-connects, travel happy

That's it. The setup takes 15 minutes at home and makes the difference between a trip where everything just works and a trip where you spend half your time hunting for WiFi that loads your email.
