🍜 FOOD & DINING

How to Eat in China Without Reading Chinese (2026)

CT
City Tech Team
Updated July 24, 2026 · 9 min read

You walk into a restaurant. The host seats you and says something in Chinese. You nod. They point at a QR code stuck to the corner of the table and walk away. There is no paper menu. There is no English. Nobody is coming back to take your order. This is the single most disorienting moment for most first-time visitors, and it happens three times a day.

Once you understand how QR-code ordering works — which takes exactly one meal — eating in China becomes the easiest, cheapest, and most enjoyable part of your trip. This guide walks through how to pick a restaurant, how to order when you can't read anything, how to pay, how much to tip (nothing, usually), and the dishes you can safely order blind.

Step 0 — Picking a restaurant

Not all restaurants are equal when you don't read Chinese. Here's how to choose well.

Your best friend: Dianping (大众点评)

Dianping is China's Yelp fused with Google Maps and food delivery. The interface is Chinese, but you don't need to read it to use it. Open the app, tap the location icon near you, and browse restaurants by photo. Every listing has dozens of real diner photos of actual food — you'll see what dishes look like before you commit. Tap a photo of food that looks good, show the screen to a taxi driver, and go.

Look for these markers next to the restaurant name:

  • ⭐ 4.5+ rating with 100+ reviews: reliably good.
  • ¥ or ¥¥ = cheap to mid-range; ¥¥¥ = upscale.
  • Top-10-dishes section: the most-ordered items, pre-vetted by hundreds of locals.
💡 The most reliable filter
At mealtime, look for restaurants packed with locals. A full restaurant in China means the food is good and cheap. Empty restaurants are empty for a reason. This beats every review.

Types of restaurants and how hard they are

🍜

Noodle / dumpling shops

Easiest. Often have picture menus. Point and hold up fingers. ¥15-40 per person.
🥘

Local sit-down restaurants

QR-code ordering, Chinese-only menus. Use the camera-translate trick below. ¥40-100 per person.
🔥

Hot pot / BBQ

Fun, social. Staff often help first-timers. Some have picture menus. ¥80-150 per person.
🍱

Food courts / malls

Each stall has display food. Point to what you want. Easy and cheap. ¥20-50 per person.

Step 1 — The QR code ritual

On the table, or stuck to the wall, or printed on the placemat, you'll see a QR code. This is the menu and ordering system. No waiter is coming. Here's the drill:

1
Open Alipay or WeChat, tap "Scan"

Use the in-app scanner, not your phone camera. It opens the menu as a mini-program.

2
Point at the QR code

The menu opens. It's usually 100% Chinese. Don't panic.

3
Translate with Google Translate camera

Download the Chinese offline pack before your trip. Open Google Translate, select Chinese → English, tap the camera icon, point at your screen showing the Chinese menu. It overlays English in real time. Translations are wonky but you'll know what things are — "red-braised pork," "scrambled eggs with tomato," "hand-pulled noodles."

4
Tap "+" to add dishes to your cart

Most menus have small photos next to items — even without translation, pick by picture. Tap + to add.

5
Tap the cart and "submit order"

The submit button says 提交订单. Pay via Alipay/WeChat on the spot. Food starts arriving in 5-15 minutes.

ℹ️ Table number
Some QR systems ask for your table number (桌号) before you can order. It's on a stand or printed on the table — usually a two-digit number. If you can't find it, wave at a staff member and say "zhuo hao?" (roughly "jwoh how?") while looking confused — they'll point.

Step 2 — When the menu has no photos

Some older restaurants have text-only menus. The camera-translate trick still works but is slower. Three hacks:

The picture walk

Stand up, walk to a table that just got food, point politely at a dish you like, give a thumbs-up to the waiter. In casual places this is completely normal — staff find it helpful and often funny.

Dianping dish photos

Look up the restaurant on Dianping, scroll to food photos, show one to the staff. The dishes almost always match.

Set meal cop-out

Look for "套餐" (set meal / combo) — often A/B/C sets with a clear price. Usually a good deal and zero decisions.

Step 3 — Paying the bill

In most mid-range and small restaurants you already paid through QR ordering before the food arrived — no bill at the end.

At nicer places or if you added items mid-meal, ask for the bill by saying "mai dan" (my dahn, "close the bill," universal across China). They'll bring a slip with a QR code. Scan, pay via Alipay/WeChat, done. Cash works at old-school places.

Tipping: just don't

There is no tipping culture in China. Nobody tips at restaurants, taxis, or hotels. Leaving extra money confuses staff — they'll often chase you down to return it. Exception: high-end international hotels may add a service charge automatically. Tour guides/private drivers may appreciate a tip for exceptional service, but it's not expected.

Dishes to order when you have no idea

These exist everywhere, are universally loved, and are basically impossible to get wrong:

  • 西红柿炒鸡蛋 (xīhóngshì chǎo jīdàn) — scrambled eggs with tomato. Sounds too simple; it's everyone's comfort food.
  • 宫保鸡丁 (gōngbǎo jīdīng) — Kung Pao chicken. Peanuts, diced chicken, chili, slightly sweet.
  • 红烧肉 (hóngshāo ròu) — red-braised pork belly. Rich, sweet, fall-apart tender.
  • 麻婆豆腐 (mápó dòufu) — mapo tofu. Spicy, numbing Sichuan classic.
  • 炒饭 (chǎofàn) — fried rice. The universal safe order.
  • 面条 (miàntiáo) — noodles. Order "niúròu miàn" (beef noodles) or "zhàjiàng miàn" (minced pork noodles) if you can pronounce them.
  • 饺子 (jiǎozi) — dumplings. Usually pork, cabbage, or chive. Boiled (shuǐjiǎo) or pan-fried (guōtiē).
  • 青菜 (qīngcài) — leafy green vegetables, usually garlic-fried. A healthy counterpoint.

If you eat beef or pork only, watch out for these three characters — they mean you might get unexpected ingredients:

  • 辣 (là) = spicy. The little chili icon next to a dish means it's hot.
  • 素 (sù) = vegetarian.
  • 花生 (huāshēng) = peanuts (common allergy warning).
⚠️ Allergies and dietary restrictions
Serious food allergies are hard to communicate at small restaurants. Carry a printed card in Chinese explaining your allergy (there are free templates online) or eat at larger hotels/restaurants where English-speaking staff can confirm ingredients. "Gluten-free" is not really a recognized concept — stick to rice dishes and avoid soy-sauce-heavy food.

Street food and small vendors

Street stalls and night markets are some of the best eating in China. The protocol is simple: point at what you want, hold up fingers for quantity, pay with Alipay (most stalls have a printed QR code on the cart). ¥10-30 gets you full. If you're nervous about hygiene, follow the same local-full rule — long lines form at stalls that haven't made anyone sick.

Drinks

  • Hot water (热水 rèshuǐ) is free and offered everywhere. Yes, even in summer. It's a cultural thing — just say no if you don't want it.
  • Beer (啤酒 píjiǔ) — local Tsingtao (青岛) is ubiquitous and cheap, ¥5-15 a bottle.
  • Bubble tea / boba (奶茶 nǎichá) — a national obsession. Chains like HeyTea, Mixue, and Chagee have picture menus with English on some. 10-25 yuan.
  • Soda — Coke, Sprite, Fanta are everywhere (slightly sweeter than in the West).

Final tip: be the polite foreigner

A few words go a long way:

  • 你好 (nǐ hǎo) — hello
  • 谢谢 (xièxie) — thank you
  • 这个 (zhège) — "this one" (point and say zhège, works for most things)
  • 买单 (mǎidān) — the bill, please
  • 不要辣 (bú yào là) — no spice (useful for chili-free eaters)

Even clumsy pronunciation gets a smile. Locals love it when a foreigner tries, and a little warmth goes a long way when you can't read a single character on the menu.

Eat well. Point at things. Don't fear the QR code — it's actually faster and easier than flagging down a waiter. After a day or two, you'll be ordering like you've lived here for years.
