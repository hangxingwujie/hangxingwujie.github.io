How to Book China Attraction Tickets as a Foreigner (2026)

CT
City Tech Team
Updated July 24, 2026 · 8 min read

You flew 12 hours to see the Forbidden City. You arrive at the gate. The guard says "yuyue" (预约 — reservation). You don't have one. The ticket window is closed. No paper tickets are sold on-site. The next available slot is three days from now — and the booking page only accepts Chinese ID numbers. You walk back to your hotel.

This exact scenario plays out daily at China's most famous sites. The reservation system is mandatory and built for ID-card holders. But it's solvable. Here's how to book every major site.

ℹ️ Real-name ticketing
Since 2019-2020, virtually all major Chinese attractions require real-name reservations — you book a time slot with an ID number, and enter by scanning your passport or a QR code at the gate. No reservation = no entry. This applies to the Forbidden City, major Great Wall sections, Terracotta Warriors, Zhangjiajie, and most top-tier sites.

Option A: Trip.com (recommended)

Trip.com accepts foreign passports directly, takes international cards, and handles the backend ID translation.

1
Open Trip.com → Attractions & Tours

Search in English: "Forbidden City," "Mutianyu Great Wall," "Terracotta Warriors."

2
Choose a date and time slot

AM and PM slots. Morning sells out first. Book 3-7 days ahead for popular sites, 2 weeks for Forbidden City.

3
Enter passport details exactly as printed

Name, passport number, nationality, date of birth.

4
Pay with international card or Alipay

Receive a QR code voucher by email. At the gate, use the "Internet Ticket" (网络票) channel — not general admission. Scan your QR code, present your passport, entry granted.

💡 Forbidden City timing
Tickets release 7 days in advance at 8 PM Beijing time. Peak-season weekend slots sell out in minutes. Set an alarm. If Trip.com shows sold out, check back at 8 PM Beijing for the next day's release — allocations refresh daily.

Option B: Official WeChat Mini-Programs

Most major sites have their own WeChat mini-program. Locals use these, and they often have more slots than Trip.com. The catch: Chinese interface, and sometimes Chinese-phone-number-only.

1
Search the attraction name in Chinese in WeChat

故宫博物院 (Forbidden City), 八达岭长城 (Badaling), 兵马俑 (Terracotta Warriors). The official mini-program appears with a blue "官方" badge.

2
Tap "预约" (reservation)

Select date, time slot, ticket type. Google Translate camera reads the options.

3
Switch ID type to "护照" (passport)

Most mini-programs default to "身份证" (Chinese ID). Look for a dropdown or "其他证件" (other ID) option. If there's no passport option, the system doesn't accept foreign IDs — use Trip.com instead.

4
Pay with WeChat Pay

Your foreign card linked to WeChat Pay works.

⚠️ Passport number rejected — three workarounds
(1) Use Trip.com — they handle ID translation on the backend. (2) Visit the ticket office in person 1-3 days early — many sites have a manual foreign-passport counter. (3) Ask your hotel concierge — international-brand concierges book tickets weekly and often have direct lines to attraction offices.

Option C: On-site ticket office

Some sites still sell on-site, but never count on it. Mutianyu Great Wall, Summer Palace, Temple of Heaven, and most city parks offer on-site tickets. The Forbidden City has zero on-site tickets — online reservation only, no exceptions. Terracotta Warriors and Zhangjiajie strongly recommend online booking.

At the gate: what to bring

  1. Your physical passport. The gate scanner reads it to verify your ticket identity.
  2. The Trip.com QR code, screenshot offline. Mobile data at crowded gates is slow.
  3. Arrive 15-30 minutes before your slot. Missing your window voids the ticket.
🎯 Concierge shortcut
If online booking rejects your passport AND Trip.com is sold out, go to your hotel concierge the day you arrive. International-brand hotel concierges book tickets daily and have direct lines to attraction offices. They can often secure slots that appear fully booked online.

Book your attraction tickets the same day you book your flights. The system is frustrating the first time, but Trip.com makes it manageable. After three or four sites, it becomes second nature.
