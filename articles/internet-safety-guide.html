Internet Safety for Foreign Travelers in China (2026)

CT
City Tech Team
Updated July 24, 2026 · 7 min read

China is one of the safest countries in the world for physical safety — violent crime against tourists is almost non-existent. The digital world is a different story. Public WiFi networks in busy areas, QR code scams near tourist sites, and fake apps designed to steal payment info are the real threats you'll face. None of them are exotic or China-specific — they're the same threats travelers face anywhere — but they're common enough here that forewarned is useful.

Public WiFi: use it, but protect yourself

Free WiFi is everywhere in China: airports, metro stations, malls, cafes, hotels. The convenience is real. The risk is that unsecured networks can expose your browsing to anyone on the same network.

⚠️ Public WiFi rule
Never log into banking, email, or payment apps on unsecured public WiFi without a connectivity app running. If you're at a cafe on their free WiFi without protection, stick to browsing. Switch to your eSIM mobile data for anything involving passwords or money.
  • Airport / hotel WiFi: Generally safe as a connection point, but not private. International hotel chains typically have better network security than cafes.
  • Cafe / restaurant / mall WiFi: Least secure. Sometimes requires a Chinese phone number for SMS verification. Assume it's an open network.
  • Use a connectivity app on public WiFi: A commercial connectivity app encrypts your data even on open networks. Our connectivity guide covers which ones work.

QR code scams: what to watch for

QR codes are ubiquitous in China — you scan them to order food, pay, enter venues, and follow official accounts. This makes them a natural attack vector. The most common tourist scam:

  1. Sticker-over QR scam: A fake QR sticker is placed over a real one. For example, a scam sticker on a bike-share bicycle or a parking payment sign. You scan it, think you're paying for the legitimate service, but the money goes to the scammer — and sometimes the link also downloads malware.
  2. Fake "free WiFi" QR codes: A QR code on a sticker says "Free WiFi" near a tourist site. You scan it, connect to a network controlled by the scammer, and they capture anything you type.

How to stay safe:

  • Scan QR codes from within apps you trust (Alipay, WeChat, DiDi). These apps validate the link before opening it.
  • Don't scan random QR codes stuck to walls, lamp posts, or taped over other codes.
  • If a QR code opens a payment page asking for more information than usual (card PIN, CVV again, full ID details), close it immediately.
  • Bike-share and shared-power-bank QR codes are the most commonly tampered with in tourist areas — scan these through Alipay's scanner rather than your camera app.

Fake apps and "too good to be true" deals

Downloading apps from unofficial sources or clicking suspicious links is the fastest way to compromise your phone in any country. In China, two patterns are especially common:

  • Fake DiDi / Alipay / WeChat apps: Scammers create app-store listings or APK download pages that look like the real thing. Download only from the official Apple App Store or Google Play. If an app has fewer than 1,000 reviews and claims to be Alipay, it's fake.
  • "Free tour" or "free gift" links: A friendly stranger approaches you near a tourist site, shows you a QR code, and says you'll get a free drink or tour if you scan it. The link asks for your WeChat login, and your account is stolen. The defense is simple: never scan QR codes from strangers on the street.
  • Tea house / art gallery invitation scam: Two well-dressed people invite you to a "cultural experience" nearby. You go, drink tea, and are presented with a bill for ¥1,000-5,000. The QR code you scan to pay is a legitimate payment — the scam is the price, not the technology. Say "no thank you" and walk away.

Phone and device security

  • Enable a PIN, Face ID, or fingerprint lock. Your phone is your wallet in China — if someone unlocks it, they can make payments through your Alipay without a password (for small amounts).
  • Keep your phone physically close. Phone theft is rare in China compared to some European cities, but crowded metro cars and tourist-site queues are the riskiest environments.
  • Install OS and app updates before you travel. You won't reliably download large updates on Chinese networks.
  • Use "Find My Device" features. Know how to remotely lock or wipe your phone if it's lost.

Data privacy: what you should know

When you use Chinese apps (Alipay, WeChat, DiDi, Meituan), you share personal data — passport scans, real-time location, payment history — as part of the services. This is functionally identical to how Uber knows your location or how PayPal knows your card details. The apps require these permissions to work.

  • Alipay / WeChat Pay require passport scans to verify identity under Chinese financial regulations. This is not optional — without it, you can't pay.
  • DiDi needs your real-time location to dispatch a car. It's GPS data, the same as Uber, Bolt, or any ride-hailing app anywhere.
  • Public WiFi portals sometimes require a Chinese phone number for an SMS code. Connect through a commercial connectivity app instead if you prefer not to share your number.
🛡️ Practical privacy steps
Use a commercial connectivity app on public networks. Keep your phone locked with biometrics. Don't share your passport number with unverified apps or strangers. The privacy exposure in Chinese apps is comparable to what you already share with Google, Apple, Meta, Uber, and your bank — the apps are simply different apps. The most effective privacy tool for any traveler in any country is awareness of what data each app asks for and why.

Quick safety checklist

  1. Install a connectivity app and eSIM before departure — both protect your data in different ways.
  2. Download apps only from official app stores (App Store / Google Play).
  3. Scan QR codes from within Alipay/WeChat, not your phone camera.
  4. Lock your phone with biometrics — it's your payment method in China.
  5. Never scan QR codes from strangers on the street.
  6. Don't log into banking or payment apps on unsecured public WiFi without a connectivity app running.
  7. Update your phone OS and apps before you fly.

China is a country where the digital world is more advanced and more integrated into daily life than almost anywhere else — and the safety risks are the same digital risks every traveler faces anywhere. Public WiFi requires caution, QR codes are what links and email attachments used to be, and your phone is your wallet. Treat it that way, and you'll have an overwhelmingly safe trip.
