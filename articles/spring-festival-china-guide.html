Spring Festival in China 2027: A Traveler's Guide to Chinese New Year

CT
City Tech Team
Updated November 9, 2026 · 11 min read

Chinese New Year — Chūnjié (春节), Spring Festival — is the largest annual human migration on earth: roughly three billion passenger trips in forty days, as everyone in the country goes home at once. It is also the single best two weeks of the year to see China behaving like itself. Red everywhere, firecrackers at midnight, families eating a table of food they spent three days cooking, and — for the first time all year — nobody in a hurry to get to work.

It is also the most logistically hostile fortnight of the year. Half the restaurants are shut, train tickets vanish in under a minute, and arriving without a plan means watching the celebration from a hotel window. This guide covers both halves: the traditions worth going out of your way for, and the mechanics that let you actually reach them.

The 60-second version

🔍 If you only read one box
When: New Year's Day falls on February 6, 2027 (Year of the Goat). New Year's Eve is February 5. Festivities run to the Lantern Festival on February 20.
Best window: arrive 2–3 days before New Year's Eve (Feb 2–5) to see the preparation, stay through the first week. Avoid arriving on New Year's Eve — it is the busiest travel day of the year.
Biggest trap: you cannot casually book intercity trains or a decent hotel in the final two weeks of December.
Where: mid-size cities and historic towns beat Beijing and Shanghai for atmosphere — see the city picks.
Cost: expect 1.5–3x normal hotel and flight prices in the peak window.

Dates and what happens when

The lunar calendar sets the festival, so the Gregorian date moves each year. For the next three cycles: 2027 — February 6, 2028 — January 26, 2029 — February 13.

⚠️ Verify the public holiday dates
The State Council publishes the official holiday arrangements (usually 8 days including an adjusted work weekend) roughly two months ahead. Treat the dates above as the lunar calendar facts; treat the official holiday notice as the source of truth for what's open. Look for it in December 2026.

The hard part: transport, closures, and prices

Spring Festival travel season — chūnyùn (春运) — starts 15 days before New Year's Eve and runs about 25 days after. Domestic demand is so high that normal travel logic stops applying. Three things to plan for:

  • Rail tickets evaporate. Seats for the peak three days sell out within minutes of release. Tickets typically go on sale about 15 days ahead on the 12306 app, with a waiting-list system that often comes through — but you need a Chinese ID or a compatible passport setup to use it. The practical workaround for visitors is booking through Trip.com, which sells the same seats in English with a markup and no ID friction. Book the day the window opens.
  • Flights are sold at holiday prices. Popular domestic routes can run 2–3x normal. Mid-morning and late-evening departures are cheaper; airports are calm and well-staffed during the holiday itself (everyone already left), and chaotic in the two days before New Year's Eve.
  • Small business goes dark. Independent restaurants, family shops, barbers, and small hotels shut for anywhere from three days to two weeks — the owner has gone home to another province. Chain convenience stores (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson), large malls, chain restaurants, and hotel restaurants stay open. Big-city downtowns are reliably fine; small towns are not.

The traditions worth seeking out

The reunion dinner

New Year's Eve dinner is the emotional centre of the festival — the one meal everyone travels across the country for. The dishes are puns as much as food: fish (鱼, yú) for surplus, dumplings (饺子, jiǎozi) shaped like ancient silver ingots in the north, rice cakes (年糕) for rising fortune in the south, spring rolls for wealth, and a whole chicken with head and feet on for completeness. Not finishing the fish is the point — leftovers mean abundance.

💡 How to actually get a New Year's Eve dinner
Hotel restaurants, chain restaurants, and large shopping-mall restaurants all run set Spring Festival menus, and they sell out — reserve three to four weeks ahead. If you're invited into a family home, that is the real experience: bring fruit, tea, or a bottle of baijiu, and expect to be fed until you physically cannot continue.

Temple fairs

The classic public celebration. A miàohuì (庙会) is a temporary fairground around a temple: skewer stalls, sugar-figure makers, paper-cutting sellers, acrobats, lottery wheels, and a stage with opera or drumming. Loud, crowded, excellent. Most run from New Year's Day to around the fifth or sixth day, daytime only, and entry is usually a few yuan or free.

  • Beijing: Ditan and Longtanhu parks hold the biggest, most traditional fairs.
  • Shanghai: Yuyuan Garden's lantern fair in the old town — as much a light show as a market.
  • Chengdu: Wuhou Shrine fair, plus Jinli Street's food stalls at full intensity.
  • Guangzhou: the flower markets — a distinct Cantonese tradition where everyone buys a flowering tree for the house.

Firecrackers, fireworks, and what you can legally do

Here is the honest answer that most guides skip: central districts of most large cities have banned private fireworks for years. Beijing's fifth ring road, Shanghai's outer ring, and similar zones in most tier-1 and tier-2 cities enforce it. What you'll still find: official municipal light shows and drone displays, theme-park fireworks, and — if you leave the big cities — a genuine, hour-long, ear-ringing barrage in towns, villages, and ancient towns where enforcement is thin.

If seeing real firecrackers matters to you, spend New Year's Eve somewhere like Pingyao, a Fujian coastal town, a Guangdong village, or anywhere in Hunan or Sichuan outside the provincial capital. Do not buy and set off fireworks yourself — restrictions vary street by street and the fines fall on the buyer.

Red envelopes and household visits

Married adults hand out red envelopes (hóngbāo) to children, younger relatives, and unmarried juniors — typically ¥100–¥500 for family, less for acquaintances. Money is given in even amounts but never in fours (四, sì, sounds like death); eights and sixes are lucky. Digital red packets via WeChat and Alipay have largely replaced cash among younger people.

You, as a visitor, are not expected to give them. If you're invited to someone's home, you'll be offered tea, snacks, and a plate of melon seeds within thirty seconds of arriving. Take one of everything. Refusing food outright is the one real faux pas.

Lion dances, drumming, and the small stuff

Look for lion dances outside restaurants and shops in the first three days — troupes go door to door, the shop owner ties a red envelope to a lettuce hung over the entrance, and the lion "eats" it. Cantonese communities do this best (Guangzhou, Foshan, Hong Kong, Macao). In Chaoshan, in the east of Guangdong, the Yingge dance — a rhythmic, stomping war dance done in painted faces — has become one of the most photographed things in China. In the northwest, look for shèhuǒ village processions in Shaanxi and Shanxi.

Where to go

The trade-off is straightforward: the biggest cities are the easiest to survive and the least atmospheric; the smallest towns are magnificent and hardest to navigate without Chinese. The sweet spot is a mid-size historic city.

💡 The contrarian move
January and early February are the only weeks when China's headline attractions are not overrun. If you've skipped the Great Wall or the Terracotta Army because of crowds, the three days after New Year's Day — when everyone is at home eating leftovers — is your window. Book attraction slots in advance anyway; capacity limits still apply. See booking tickets with a passport.

Etiquette that actually matters

  • Wear red, avoid all-white. Red is luck; head-to-toe white or black reads as funeral dress. This is the one rule almost every visitor breaks.
  • Don't sweep or take out the rubbish on New Year's Day. You'd be sweeping the year's good fortune out the door. If you're a guest, don't touch the broom.
  • Gift taboos: no clocks (送钟 sounds like "attending a funeral"), no umbrellas ("separation"), no shoes ("evil"), no sharp objects ("severing ties"), no white flowers. Fruit, tea, good liquor, and pastries are always safe. Give and receive gifts with both hands.
  • Numbers: avoid four. Favour six, eight, and nine. Red-envelope amounts should be even.
  • If you break a glass, say "suì suì píng ān" (岁岁平安) — "peace year after year" — the sound of "broken" doubles as "year". Locals will be delighted you know it.
  • Photography: temple fairs and street scenes are fair game. Photographing people's front doors, altars, or a family's dinner table without asking is not. A raised camera and a questioning look gets you a yes more often than you'd expect.
  • Don't say "Happy New Year" too early — it's a New Year's Day greeting, not a December one. Xīnnián kuàilè (新年快乐, happy new year) or gōngxǐ fācái (恭喜发财, "may you prosper") on the day itself.

The digital side still applies

Nothing about the festival suspends the basics — you still need a working data connection and a payment app before you land. A few holiday-specific wrinkles:

  • Payments: QR payment works exactly as normal and is more essential than usual, since many small vendors that would take cash are shut. If you haven't set it up, do it first — Alipay for foreigners.
  • Ride-hailing: DiDi runs, but driver supply drops sharply on New Year's Eve and Day, and platforms add a Spring Festival surcharge. Expect longer waits and 10–30% higher fares. Metro service is normal, and often extended on New Year's Eve.
  • Food delivery: Meituan and Ele.me operate throughout, with fewer riders and higher delivery fees. Order earlier than usual.
  • Connectivity: hotel Wi-Fi and mobile data behave normally. If you're relying on an eSIM or roaming, sort it before you fly — roaming vs eSIM vs local SIM.
  • Hotels: the foreign-guest licensing rule still applies and matters more when everything else is full. See hotel check-in for foreigners.

A realistic eight-day plan

1
Feb 3 – arrive and acclimatise

Fly in three days before New Year's Eve. Markets are full of red couplets, the city is calm, and you can still get a table anywhere.

2
Feb 4 – markets and preparation

Wander a flower market or New Year goods street. Buy a red couplet for your hotel door — the staff will enjoy it.

3
Feb 5 – New Year's Eve

Booked dinner at 6pm. CCTV gala on in the background. Be outside at midnight for the firecrackers, wherever they're permitted.

4
Feb 6 – New Year's Day

Expect a ghost town. Visit a temple in the morning — this is when locals go — and eat wherever is open. Don't plan a shopping day.

5
Feb 7 – temple fair

Full day at the biggest fair in reach: food, acrobatics, paper cuts, opera stage.

6
Feb 8 – lion dance morning

Downtown commercial streets, late morning. Troupes hit shops door to door for a few hours only.

7
Feb 9 – move cities

Rail is still busy but manageable by day five. Head to the second city for the back half.

8
Feb 10 – the empty-tourist-site window

Great Wall, Terracotta Army, Forbidden City — genuinely quiet. Reserve slots online anyway.

Before you book

  1. Decide the city first, then the dates — availability drives this, not preference.
  2. Book long-distance rail the day the 15-day window opens; book hotels 6–8 weeks out.
  3. Reserve New Year's Eve dinner three to four weeks ahead.
  4. Set up Alipay and your data plan before departure — don't arrive needing both.
  5. Pack for real winter: most of the country is 0–10°C, the north is −10 to −25°C, and southern buildings have no central heating.
  6. Download offline maps and a translation app with the Chinese pack.
  7. Check the State Council holiday notice in December for the exact closure pattern.
  8. Carry some cash for red envelopes and small vendors, even though you'll pay by QR.

Spring Festival rewards the traveller who plans like an adult and then stops planning. Book the trains, book the dinner, learn four words of Mandarin — and then let the week be as loud, red, and unhurried as it wants to be.
