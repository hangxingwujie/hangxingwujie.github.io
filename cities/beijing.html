🏯 BEIJING

Beijing: the practical guide for foreign visitors

CT
City Tech Team
Updated November 2026 · 10 min read
Best for: history, scale, first impressions Days needed: 3–4 Difficulty: moderate Peak season: Sep–Oct, Apr–May

Beijing is the most bureaucratic city in China, and that is not a complaint — it is a planning note. Almost everything worth seeing is ticketed, capped, passport-registered and closed on Mondays. Get the bookings right and Beijing delivers more than any other city in the country. Wing it and you will spend three days looking at things from the outside.

This guide covers the four things that actually decide whether your trip works: how to book, which Great Wall to choose, how to pay on the metro, and what a realistic three days looks like.

Arriving

Beijing has two airports and they are 60km apart. Check which one you booked.

  • Capital (PEK) — northeast. Terminal 3 is the main international one. The Airport Express connects T3 to Dongzhimen in about 25 minutes for ¥25, then you transfer to the metro. A taxi to the centre runs ¥100–130 and 45–70 minutes depending on traffic.
  • Daxing (PKX) — far south, opened 2019. Architecturally spectacular, genuinely inconvenient. The Daxing Airport Express reaches Caoqiao in about 20 minutes; from there the metro takes you anywhere. Taxis are ¥180–220 because of the distance.
💡 Do this on the plane, not at the airport
Have Alipay set up and your data working before you land. Airport Wi-Fi requires a Chinese phone number for the SMS code, which you do not have yet — a genuinely common first-hour deadlock. See Alipay setup and getting data.

The Forbidden City: book it or skip it

This is the one booking that decides your trip. The Palace Museum admits a fixed daily quota, sells only in advance, only online, and never at the gate. There is no ticket window to queue at.

  • When to book: tickets release seven days ahead and the popular morning slots go quickly. Book the day your window opens, especially for weekends and holidays.
  • Where: the official WeChat mini-program (故宫博物院) is the reliable route. Third-party resellers exist and add fees; the official channel is fine if you have a passport.
  • What you need: your passport details, exactly as printed. The name on the booking must match the passport you carry in.
  • Entry: you enter from the south (Meridian Gate) and exit north into Jingshan Park. You cannot go backwards through the complex.
  • Closed Mondays. Every Monday, year-round, except public holidays. This catches out a remarkable number of visitors.
⚠️ The passport queue is real
On arrival, foreign passports cannot use the automated gates. Look for the staffed lane to the side of the main turnstiles. Budget an extra 10–15 minutes and keep your booking confirmation visible on your phone.

Two hours is enough for the central axis; four hours lets you add the Treasure Gallery and the Clock Gallery, both of which are worth the small extra ticket. If you want context, the audio guide is genuinely good.

The Great Wall: three sections, three different days

"The Great Wall" is not one experience. The section you pick determines whether you get a photo with 5,000 people or a ridge to yourself.

SectionDistanceCrowdsReality
Badaling70 kmVery heavyThe famous one, accessible by high-speed train from Beijing North in 30 minutes. Restored, stepped, and shoulder-to-shoulder by 9am. Efficient rather than atmospheric.
Mutianyu90 kmModerateThe best all-round choice. Restored, dramatic ridgeline, cable car up and a toboggan down. Around 2 hours by car or direct tourist bus.
Jinshanling130 kmLightPartly unrestored, genuinely wild, a 10km ridge walk between towers. Needs a private car or organised transfer and a full day. For people who came for the wall, not the photo.
Jiankou100 kmVery lightCompletely unrestored and technically closed to visitors. Popular with hikers, official access is ambiguous. Do not plan your only wall day here.
💡 The contrarian timing
The week after Chinese New Year and the deep winter months (December–February) are the only times Badaling is genuinely quiet. Cold, clear, and photogenic in a way the summer haze never is.

Getting around: the metro does everything

Beijing's metro is 27 lines, costs ¥3–10 a ride, and is signed in English throughout. You do not need a taxi in this city.

  • Paying: the simplest method for foreigners is the QR code in Alipay — open the Transport section, activate the Beijing metro code, scan at the gate. WeChat Pay works too. Both need to be set up before you arrive.
  • Alternative: a Yikatong card from any station machine or window, ¥20 deposit plus top-up. No registration, no phone needed. Useful as a backup if your phone dies.
  • Single tickets: machines have an English option but only accept cash or QR — not foreign cards.
  • Security: every station has bag scanners and they are strict about liquids and power banks. Allow a few extra minutes at big interchanges.
  • Rush hour: 7:30–9:30am and 5:30–7pm are genuinely crushing on Lines 1, 2, 4, 5 and 10. Plan around them if you can.
  • Taxis and DiDi: work fine, but distances are large and traffic is heavy. The metro is usually faster. See using DiDi in English.

A realistic three days

1
Day 1 — the axis

Tiananmen Square (passport security check) → Forbidden City south to north, booked in advance → exit into Jingshan Park for the view back over the roofs → evening in the hutongs around Nanluoguxiang or Shichahai.

2
Day 2 — the wall

Mutianyu, leaving the hotel by 7am to beat both the traffic and the tour buses. Back in the city by mid-afternoon; Peking duck for dinner, booked ahead.

3
Day 3 — temples and parks

Temple of Heaven in the morning when locals are doing tai chi and dancing, then Lama Temple or the Confucius Temple, then 798 art district if you want contemporary contrast.

4
Spare day

Summer Palace, or a day trip to the Ming Tombs. Or slow down — Beijing rewards walking more than ticking off.

What to eat

  • Peking duck — the actual ritual, carved at the table. Book ahead; the famous places fill up days out. Expect ¥200–400 for two.
  • Jianbing — the breakfast crepe from a street cart, ¥8–12. The single best cheap meal in the city.
  • Zhajiangmian — thick wheat noodles with fermented bean sauce. A Beijing staple, not a tourist dish.
  • Hotpot — the northern copper-pot style with lamb, distinct from the Sichuan version.
  • Where: the hutong neighbourhoods north of the Forbidden City have the best mix of quality and price. Mall restaurants are reliable but interchangeable.
⚠️ Three practical warnings
Air quality varies by day — check an AQI app in the morning and move outdoor plans if it is bad. Winter is genuinely cold (−10°C is normal in January) and the wind on the wall is brutal. Mondays close the Forbidden City, the National Museum, and most major sights at once — never make Monday your museum day.

Beijing checklist

  1. Book the Forbidden City the day the 7-day window opens. Not on arrival.
  2. Confirm which airport you fly into — PEK and PKX are not interchangeable.
  3. Set up Alipay metro QR before you land.
  4. Pick your Great Wall section deliberately, not by whatever the hotel offers.
  5. Book Peking duck one to two days ahead.
  6. Avoid planning anything ticketed on a Monday.
  7. Carry your physical passport every day — you need it for security checks at Tiananmen and major sights.
