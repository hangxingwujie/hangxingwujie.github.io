🌆 SHANGHAI

Shanghai: the easiest place to start in China

CT
City Tech Team
Updated November 2026 · 9 min read
Best for: a soft landing Days needed: 2–3 Difficulty: low Peak season: Mar–May, Sep–Nov

If this is your first trip to mainland China, start here. Not because Shanghai is the most Chinese city — it is emphatically not — but because it is the least punishing place to make your beginner mistakes. Cards get accepted. Staff speak some English. The metro is signed properly. Nothing critical is shut on a Monday.

That makes Shanghai a good place to spend your first 48 hours sorting out Alipay, a data connection, and the rhythm of the place, before heading somewhere that rewards more preparation.

Two airports, one easy decision

  • Pudong (PVG) — most international flights. 30km east of the city, which is further than it looks.
  • Hongqiao (SHA) — domestic and some regional international flights. Much closer, and attached to Hongqiao Railway Station, which is a major high-speed rail hub. If you are flying in from another Chinese city, this is where you will land.

💡 The maglev does not go downtown
It stops at Longyang Road, still 8km from the centre. Factor in a metro transfer. It is a brilliant 8 minutes and a poor door-to-door solution — take it for the fun, not the efficiency.

The metro is the city

Twenty lines, English signage everywhere, ¥3–9 a ride, and stations every few minutes. You will almost never need a taxi inside the outer ring.

  • Paying: the Alipay transport QR is the simplest foreigner route — activate the Shanghai metro code and scan at the gate. WeChat Pay works identically.
  • Alternative: a Transport Card (交通卡) from any station, ¥20 deposit. No registration, works on metro, bus, ferry and taxi. The most friction-free option if you do not want to depend on a phone.
  • Single tickets: touchscreen machines have English and accept cash and QR, but not foreign cards.
  • Line 2 is the workhorse: it connects both airports, Hongqiao Railway Station, People's Square, the Bund area and Lujiazui. If you learn one line, learn that one.
  • Rush hour is 7:30–9:30am and 5:30–7pm. Line 2 and Line 9 are genuinely unpleasant. Shift your day by an hour and the city opens up.

What to book, and what not to bother with

  • Shanghai Museum — free, but requires an advance online reservation with your passport. Book a few days out. The East branch in Pudong is newer and easier to get into.
  • Shanghai Tower observation deck — book online for a small discount; go within an hour of sunset for both daylight and lights.
  • Yu Garden — the garden itself is ticketed and worth it; the surrounding bazaar is free and best at night when the lanterns are lit.
  • The Bund — no booking, no ticket, open 24 hours. Go at 6:30am for the tai chi and no crowds, or after 9pm when the buildings are lit and the tour groups have gone.
  • Water towns — Zhujiajiao is the closest (1 hour) and the most visited. Seven Treasures (Qibao) is smaller. Both are day trips, not destinations.
⚠️ Skip the "river cruise" upsell
The Huangpu dinner cruises are heavily marketed and consistently mediocre for the price. The ¥2 public ferry across the river at Jinling East Road gives you the same skyline for the price of a coffee, and locals actually use it.

Two days, realistically

1
Day 1 morning — the old city

Yu Garden early (opens 9am, go straight there), then the bazaar and the City God Temple area. Cross to the Bund and walk north to Waibaidu Bridge.

2
Day 1 afternoon — the concession

The Former French Concession around Wukang Road, Anfu Road and Fuxing Park. Plane trees, cafés, independent shops. This is where Shanghai actually lives.

3
Day 1 evening — the skyline

Ferry across to Lujiazui, then Shanghai Tower or the Riverside promenade for the view back at the Bund. Dinner in Lujiazui or back across the river.

4
Day 2 — pick one axis

Either museums and Jing'an (Shanghai Museum, then the Jing'an Temple area), or a water town day trip, or the 1933 Old Millfun / M50 art district for something contemporary.

5
Spare half-day

Zhujiajiao water town, or a half-day run to Suzhou by high-speed rail (23 minutes) if you want classical gardens without the Shanghai price tag.

What to eat

  • Xiaolongbao — soup dumplings. The famous chains are fine; the best are neighbourhood shops with plastic stools.
  • Shengjianbao — pan-fried pork buns, the Shanghai breakfast. Crisp bottom, hot soup inside. Eat them carefully.
  • Scallion oil noodles (葱油拌面) — ¥15, deceptively simple, the definitive local cheap meal.
  • Red-braised pork (红烧肉) — sweet, dark, unapologetically rich. The Shanghainese signature dish.
  • Where: Huanghe Road and the lanes off Nanjing West Road for old-school; the French Concession for modern Shanghai cooking. Avoid the restaurant strip on the Bund itself.

Shanghai checklist

  1. Confirm whether you land at Pudong or Hongqiao — the transfer is completely different.
  2. Set up Alipay before you fly; it is accepted almost everywhere here.
  3. Reserve the Shanghai Museum online a few days ahead.
  4. Learn Line 2. It covers most of what you want.
  5. See the Bund at 6:30am or after 9pm, not at midday.
  6. Take the ¥2 ferry instead of the dinner cruise.
  7. If continuing by rail, Hongqiao Railway Station is the main hub — book with these instructions.
